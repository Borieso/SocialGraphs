1 · Why language?

Language is a very informative record of human behavior. It tells us what people talk about, which distinctions they make, and how they describe the world around them. There is a long-standing idea in linguistics, usually called linguistic relativity, that the language we use can shape the way we perceive, think about, and conceptualize the world.

Hand me the beer north of you. One of my favorite examples of how the language we speak can influence human cognition comes from Kuuk Thaayorre, a language spoken at Pormpuraaw in northern Australia. In Danish or English, if I ask you to hand me something, I might say "the beer on your right". However, while we use relative spatial concepts such as left and right, Kuuk Thaayorre relies on absolute directions such as north, south, east, and west. So a speaker might instead describe an object as being southwest of you. This means that speakers have to keep track of their absolute orientation in a way that most Danish or English speakers rarely need to. The fact that cardinal directions are used in everyday spatial reference already means that speakers need to maintain a strong sense of their orientation.

But it is even deeper! In a study by Gaby (2012), participants were asked to arrange image sequences with a natural temporal order, such as a person growing older. English speakers tended to arrange the sequence from left to right. Kuuk Thaayorre speakers, however, tended to arrange it from east to west. When they were seated facing another direction, the direction of the sequence changed as well. The way they sequence things also follows the compass rather than the table they were seated at. You can find the study here: Gaby (2012).

So why am I telling you this? Because language is data about people. Words record what people notice, how they group things, and how they communicate those groupings to other people. The example above is striking, but it's just the tip of the iceberg. Similar examples exist for color, space, time, and many other parts of cognition. And (lucky for us) the internet is the source of a fair bit of language, which means we can use it to study human behavior at a scale that would otherwise be impossible.

2 · From raw text to tokens

Tokenization. Sounds easy. Isn't easy. The loop above already depends on a word we have not properly defined, namely a token.

A computer does not begin with words. It begins with a string of characters. Take this sentence:

Iron Man wasn't in New York.

To the computer, that is just a sequence of characters. Before we can count words, build a vocabulary, calculate frequencies, or feed the text into a model, we need to decide where one piece of text ends and the next begins. That process is tokenization; the units that come out are tokens.

You might think to yourselves, well, this is easy. This is why we have spaces between words!

But even that simple approach already makes a choice. In Python we could split on such spaces:

That's actually a tokenizer, just a very simple one. Notice what happened. wasn't stayed together, while the full stop stayed attached to York.. A more language-aware tokenizer might instead produce

['Iron', 'Man', 'was', "n't", 'in', 'New', 'York', '.']

Neither sequence is somehow "natural", and neither is objective or "right". Somebody wrote rules, or trained a model, that decided where the boundaries should go.

Always be aware of your modeling choices. This is why tokenization sounds much easier than it is. Is um a token? Is New York one token or two? What about 'Apple's', 'weren't', an emoji, a URL, a hashtag, or punctuation? There is no single correct answer. The useful choice depends on what you are trying to measure. As with the network half of the course, preprocessing is part of the model whether we realize it or not. So you need to argue for your choices and be transparent about them.

Getting into the weeds a little bit. A useful distinction here is between word-like tokenization and the subword tokenization used by modern language models. A library such as spaCy in Python usually gives us units that look like words and punctuation. A modern language model doesn't need to keep every possible word in a gigantic dictionary. Instead it works with a fixed vocabulary of reusable pieces. A rare word can therefore be split into smaller subword tokens that the model already knows.

So a modern pipeline looks something along the lines of

raw characters → tokenizer → token pieces → token IDs → model

The token ID is simply an integer that points to an entry in the model's vocabulary. The model never receives the word apple in the human sense. It receives one or more token IDs and learns how those IDs behave from training data.

Different tasks can reasonably tokenize the same text differently. A speech transcript may keep um and uh because hesitations carry information. A search system may normalize different forms of a word. A language model keeps punctuation and subword pieces because those units are useful for prediction.

There are also preprocessing choices that happen after tokenization. Lowercasing is one example. Sometimes Apple and apple should collapse to the same type. Sometimes the capital A is exactly the useful information. The Big Apple refers to New York City, while a big apple might be something you had for lunch. Stopword removal is another. Stopwords are the very frequent function words such as the, of, in and were, and dropping them leaves mostly the content words behind. A third is lemmatization, which maps every word form to its dictionary form, its lemma: heroes becomes hero, fighting becomes fight, and were becomes be. Every one of these choices removes information — capitalization, function words, tense and number — which may be completely fine for one analysis, but a terrible idea for another.

Try the same sentence with a subword tokenizer and you will get a different set of units. That is exactly what we expect. And just to say it one more time: the reason is that the two tokenizers were built for different jobs.

Some definitions. A token is an occurrence. A type is a distinct token form. The vocabulary is the set of types. If apple occurs ten times, that is ten tokens but one type. If Apple and apple both occur and we keep capitalization, they are two types. If we lowercase first, they become one. These counts are therefore partly properties of the corpus and partly properties of the preprocessing choices we made before counting.

There is one more reason modern models like subwords. As a corpus grows, we keep encountering new word forms, so the number of unique word types keeps growing. This empirical pattern is known as Heaps' law. A fixed subword vocabulary gives the model reusable pieces for words it has never seen as complete forms.

3 · Counting language, the old-school way

Now that we have tokens, let us do the first thing that comes to mind: count them! Before doing any complex modeling, it is always informative (and important) to look at the corpus, and yes, I know you already counted with the builder in the exercise above, but now I will take you through how to count and some of the famous statistics of language.

The habit of counting, getting a sense of the underlying data before doing complex models is similar to our approach in the network half of the course. Frankly, it's a cornerstone of how I do science in general. You would not build a complex model of a network without first calculating the number of nodes, the number of edges and the degree distribution. Visualizing it, checking for self-loops, and so on. In the same way, don't just trust a text model before you know what is actually in the text. Yes, I know this is a little bit in contrast to the logic of deep learning development, but I am pretty sure that the frontier labs also see gains when they stop feeding their models garbage.

From frequency to rank. If we sort all word types from most common to least common, we can give each word a frequency rank. Rank 1 is the most common word, rank 2 is the second most common, and so on. When people started doing this across large collections of language, they noticed a surprisingly regular pattern. A small number of words occur all the time, while a very large number occur rarely.

Nothing mysterious happens when you switch axes. You are drawing the same rank and frequency values in a different coordinate system. (This is the second example of power laws in the course, a nice connection back to week 2.) In fact, the rank-frequency plot is the CCDF from week 2 with the axes swapped: the rank of a word with frequency f is the number of words that occur at least f times. That is why, just like the CCDF, it never needs binning.

The ideal curve is useful because it gives us something simple we can compare to real language. Real text rarely sits perfectly on the mathematical line, but large collections of language often come close over much of the distribution. A nice example comes from Moby Dick. In a paper on Zipf's law, Sander Lestrade plots the word frequencies from the novel against their ranks in log-log space, and the observed points follow the expected Zipf pattern closely. Other natural-language corpora deviate more, so it is better to think of Zipf's law as a recurring empirical pattern than as a rule every corpus has to follow exactly. If you are curious, you can see the example in Lestrade (2017).

Words in context. Frequency tells us what occurs a lot, but it does not tell us how a word is being used. If power occurs 500 times, for example, those appearances could refer to political power, electrical power, superpowers, or something completely different. To understand that, we need to return to the language surrounding each occurrence.

A concordance, also called KWIC for key word in context, finds every occurrence of a target word and prints a small window of text around it. Instead of reading thousands of pages from beginning to end, we can put the target word in the middle and quickly inspect the different contexts in which it appears. This is a very simple method, but it is one I still find genuinely useful when I want to understand a new corpus.

Imagine that you downloaded every arXiv paper mentioning a particular scientific methodology (like "modularity"). Counting tells you how often the method appears and whether its use has increased over time. A concordance answers a different question because it lets you inspect what researchers actually say when they mention it. Are they introducing the method, criticizing it, comparing it with something else, or simply citing it?

NLTK's similar asks another question. Instead of showing occurrences of the target word, it searches for other words that appear in similar immediate contexts. If blue repeatedly occurs in contexts where red could also occur, the two words may be returned as similar even though the algorithm has no dictionary telling it that they are both colors. Those returned words are not guaranteed to be synonyms. The method only knows that they occupy similar positions in this particular corpus.

That distinction will become useful next week because it gives us an early version of an idea that sits behind word embeddings (more on that later). If words repeatedly occur in similar contexts, we can start to represent that contextual behavior mathematically.

Which words occur together? Concordances and similarity begin to work on how words are connected to the text around them. But there is a simpler method that captures something subtly different. New York means something different from simply observing that New and York both occur somewhere in the same corpus, and machine learning carries information that disappears when we count machine and learning independently.

The n-gram is an ultra-simple yet powerful way to look at short sequences of neighboring tokens together. A unigram contains one token, a bigram contains two, and a trigram contains three. The sequence New York City therefore contains the unigrams New, York, and City, the bigrams New York and York City, and the trigram New York City.

This also gives us one of the simplest possible language models. Imagine that we want to predict the next word. A bigram model looks only at the previous token and asks which words have followed it in the corpus before. If New is followed by York 900 times and by car 5 times, the model will assign a much higher probability to York after New. A trigram model keeps two previous tokens instead, so after seeing New York it can estimate which tokens are most likely to come next.

There is an obvious trade-off as the n-grams get longer. Longer n-grams preserve more local order and give us more context, but exact sequences also become much rarer. You may see New thousands of times and New York hundreds of times, while a particular five-word sequence occurs once. This sparsity problem is one of the reasons older n-gram language models become difficult as the context window grows.

4 · From words to documents

Old-school NLP. So far we have mostly treated the corpus as a stream of tokens. That is useful when we want to know which words occur, how frequently they occur, or which words tend to occur next to each other. As promised in the introduction, we have been working in the LLM way of thinking.

But old-school NLP is also still useful for science. Useful for understanding the world. And parts of the old-school stuff ask quite different questions. Questions about larger pieces of text. Which articles discuss the same thing? What does one network community talk about that another does not? Which character pages use unusually similar language?

For questions like these, the object we want to represent is no longer one word, but a document.

So what is a document? A document is simply the unit of text you decide to analyze. A Wikipedia page can be a document, but so can a news article, a review, a speech, everything written by one user, or all text attached to the nodes in one network community. There is no universally accepted document boundary. As with many of the choices we have made in both network science and NLP, the useful definition depends on the question you are asking.

Before doing anything clever with documents, we should (again... this is a theme) start with some simple checks and statistics. For example, how long is each document? Is one page ten times longer than the others? How many different words occur in each document? Do some pages contain large amounts of repeated boilerplate? These checks are easy, and they often explain strange results long before the model does.

Bag of Words. The simplest way to turn an entire document into numbers is a method called Bag of Words.

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple. We take the vocabulary of our corpus, give every vocabulary term its own position, and then describe each document by counting how many times those terms occur.

Often a corpus is summarized in a so-called document-term matrix X, with one row per document and one column per vocabulary term. The entry is the number of times term t appears in document d. (Some books, especially in information retrieval, use the transpose, a term-document matrix with terms as rows; the numbers are the same, only flipped.)

Before we play with the interactive version, I want to walk through the full process once. The static figure below starts with three tiny documents and follows them through tokenization, vocabulary construction and counting until they become numerical vectors in a document-term matrix. Take the first document from the figure, brains predict the future. The vocabulary for the three documents is ordered alphabetically as

brains, build, future, models, predict, the

Now go through that vocabulary one term at a time. brains occurs once in the first document, build 0 times, future once, models 0 times, predict once and the once. The first document therefore becomes the vector

[1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1]

Do the same thing for the other two documents and stack the vectors on top of one another:

That takes us to the document-term matrix. Rows are documents, columns are vocabulary terms, and every cell tells us how many times that term occurred in that document.

This is kind of amazing. I know this might seem trivial, but it's pretty great. Think about it. We started with language that a human can read and ended with a numerical representation that ordinary statistical tools can work with. You can already see why putting every document in the same vocabulary order is useful: Documents 1 and 2 share future, predict and the, while Document 3 shares brains with Document 1 and models with Document 2.

Once every document has the same set of coordinates, we can compare documents, cluster them, compare groups, or use the matrix as input to another model. You see where this is going: the words have become numbers, but we can still trace patterns in those numbers back to the text.

There is also an important limitation that is part and parcel of the name Bag of Words. We keep information about which words occur and how often they occur, while word order disappears. Consider the two sentences "brains predict models" and "models predict brains." They contain exactly the same words with exactly the same counts, so a Bag-of-Words representation gives them the same vector even though they describe different things. The example makes the loss of word order very easy to see.

As always, whether that matters depends on the question. If we want to compare what two political speeches discuss, word counts may already tell us a lot. If we want to know who did what to whom in a sentence, losing word order is a much bigger problem. This trade-off will keep coming back over the next two weeks because every representation keeps some information and throws other information away.

What happens when the corpus gets large? Our example has six vocabulary terms, so every vector contains six numbers. Real corpora are less tidy. The full Marvel pages contain about 27,000 different vocabulary terms, so every document is represented in a space with about 27,000 dimensions.

Most of those cells will be zero because one Wikipedia page uses only a small fraction of the full vocabulary. We call a matrix like this sparse. A document might conceptually look like

[0, 0, 0, 3, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0,..., 2, 0]

with zeros almost everywhere. Back in the day, we were always looking for algorithms that were fast for sparse matrices.

Constructing the doc-term matrix is another place where the preprocessing choices from Section 2 matter. Keeping punctuation, capitalization, extremely rare words, common stopwords, and different grammatical forms can change the size and contents of the vocabulary, which changes the numerical representation of every document. The matrix does not simply exist inside the raw text waiting for us to discover it. We create it through a sequence of choices — and you should be able to defend those choices.

5 · Where counting stops

Counting gets us surprisingly far. With little more than tokenization and a frequency table, we can already learn which words dominate a corpus, which words occur together, how documents differ, and which pieces of text use similar vocabularies. That is a lot of information from very simple tools, and in many research projects this type of information may already be enough to answer the question you care about.

Which pages look alike? Once every page is a Bag-of-Words vector, we can do even more cool stuff. For example, we can ask which pages point in the same direction. The standard measure is cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two vectors,

A value of 1 means that two vectors point in the same direction, and 0 means that they are at right angles, which for word counts means the two documents share no terms at all. Because we divide by the lengths, a long page and a short page that use words in the same proportions count as identical.

The explorable below does this for all 303 full Marvel pages. Pick a character and you get the ten pages whose word counts point in the most similar direction, the words that contribute most to each match, and whether the two characters are linked in the week 1 network. Start with Wolverine and raw counts. Why is Quicksilver, who is not even linked to Wolverine, his second-closest page? Look at the words behind the match, then remove the stopwords and look again. Finally, switch to closest unlinked pairs: pages that talk alike but never link to each other.

Bag-of-Words cosine between the full Wikipedia pages, with raw counts or with NLTK's English stopwords removed. The readouts compare how many of the ten textual neighbors are also network neighbors with what ten random pages would give.

Two things to take away. With raw counts almost everything looks like everything, because the, and and of dominate every vector. And once the stopwords are gone, language starts to rediscover the network: characters who link to each other also tend to be described in similar words, although nobody told the vectors about the links.

But there is one thing our counts still know almost nothing about, and that is meaning.

Word vectors. Think back to the Bag-of-Words representation. Every vocabulary term gets its own column, and the value in that column tells us how often the word occurs. This works well for keeping track of word identity and frequency, but the coordinates themselves contain no information about how the words are related.

To see that, turn the matrix on its side. Until now a document has been the thing we represent, with words as its coordinates. Ask instead what vector a single word has in this scheme, and the answer is disappointing: its own column, and nothing else. This is a change in perspective that we'll use in the rest of the language half. The thing being represented stops being the document... and becomes the word (did anyone say word embeddings?). So it is worth slowing down for one small example.

Suppose our vocabulary contains only three words:

In this toy coordinate system, dog, puppy and democracy are separate because they occupy different coordinates. If we compare those basis vectors with cosine similarity, dog is just as similar to puppy as it is to democracy:

Basis vectors are at right angles to each other, so every pair of different words gets a cosine of 0. We will spend much more time on cosine similarity next week, when the vectors themselves begin to encode context and meaning.

That is a strange description of language. As humans, we know that dog and puppy are related, while dog and democracy usually are not (well... maybe in political satire). Our representation cannot recover that relationship because we never gave it information from which the relationship could be learned.

This brings us back to another problem we have met several times this week. When we count words independently, we throw away most of their context. As we've been over a few times, a Bag-of-Words model gives the same representation to

because the same words occur the same number of times. It also has no built-in way to know whether bank refers to a financial institution or the side of a river.

Beyond the counts. N-grams helped us keep some local order, but they introduced another problem. As we make the sequence longer, exact combinations become increasingly rare. A five-word phrase gives us much more context than a single word, yet we may only see that exact phrase once in the entire corpus.

So perhaps we need to change the question. Until now we have mainly asked how often a word occurs, and next week we begin asking where this word occurs and which other words tend to appear around it.

There is a powerful idea hiding in that question. Words that occur in similar contexts often have related meanings. dog and puppy might both appear near words such as walk, pet, owner, bark and leash, while democracy will tend to appear in very different surroundings. If we record those contextual patterns, we can start to represent meaning through the company that words keep.

This is the step that takes us from counting words to vector semantics. Instead of leaving every word in an arbitrary vocabulary coordinate, we use patterns in language to build vectors whose positions tell us something about how words behave. Words used in similar contexts can then end up close together, and once language lives in that kind of space we can use geometry to measure similarity.

Essentials

This is the Learn core of the week in one place. If you can do the small examples below with a pen and explain what each representation keeps and throws away, you are ready for this week's share of Test 2.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)

methods for turning language into representations a computer can work with, then using those representations to perform a task.

Token

one occurrence produced by a tokenizer. The same type can appear as many tokens.

Type · vocabulary

a type is one distinct token form; the vocabulary is the set of types kept by the representation.

Tokenization

the decision about where one text unit ends and the next begins. Different tokenizers can reasonably split the same string differently.

Subword tokenization

representing text with reusable pieces smaller than whole words. Modern language models use fixed subword vocabularies so unfamiliar words can still be represented.

Token ID

the integer index used to look up a token or subword piece in a model's vocabulary.

Preprocessing

choices made before measurement or modeling, such as lowercasing, lemmatization, punctuation filtering and stopword removal. Every one can remove useful information.

Stopword

a very frequent function word, such as the, of or in, that many analyses drop to leave the content words.

Lemmatization

mapping each word form to its dictionary form, its lemma (heroes → hero, were → be).

Frequency · relative frequency

a raw count of how often a type occurs; relative frequency divides by the total number of tokens so differently sized corpora can be compared more sensibly.

Hapax

a type observed exactly once in the corpus.

Zipf's law

the recurring empirical pattern that word frequency falls roughly as an inverse power of frequency rank. A straight-ish log-log plot is suggestive, not a proof that a corpus follows an exact Zipf model.

Concordance / KWIC

the occurrences of a target word shown with a small window of surrounding text. It answers how is this word used here?

similar

an NLTK operation that looks for words occupying comparable immediate contexts. Similar context does not guarantee synonymy.

N-gram

a sequence of neighboring tokens. Unigrams keep one token, bigrams two, trigrams three; longer n-grams keep more local order and become sparser.

Collocation

a pair or sequence that occurs together more strongly than its individual word frequencies alone would suggest.

Document

the unit of text you choose to represent, for example one Wikipedia page, article, speech, review or user's posts.

Bag of Words

a document representation that counts vocabulary terms while discarding word order.

Document-term matrix

rows are documents, columns are vocabulary terms, and each cell contains that term's value for that document, here a count.

Sparse matrix

a matrix in which most entries are zero, as happens when a large vocabulary is shared by documents that each use only a small part of it.
