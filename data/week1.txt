1 · Why networks?

Here is the key idea in the course: for many real-world systems, the pattern of connections can give us a deeper understanding of how the system works and changes than if we simply look at the individual parts. Who infects whom, which idea spreads, why some Wikipedia pages are famous and most are invisible, how a blackout cascades across a continent. We can't get any of that one component at a time. But if we start from the network, we can begin to see the patterns that shape those processes. And we can find patterns that reveal similarities between systems that we thought wouldn't be related at all.

The idea that networks are important has a history, running from a coffee-room puzzle about bridges in Königsberg to the moment physicists collided with sociologists in the late 1990s. It is a story that I am deeply connected to... so that is where we start.

Uncle Sune (young Sune in fact) tells his version of the story. The founding myths of network science: Euler's bridges, random graphs, small worlds, scale-free networks.

Notice what happened in my talk. For two hundred years, graph theory was mathematics — beautiful, exact. Then, around the turn of the millennium, the data began arriving. At first as a trickle, then a tsunami: email logs, collaboration records, the web itself. Suddenly the same key set of measurements could be run on networks of millions of nodes from wildly different domains.

And we (the scientists) kept finding the same structural patterns. That quest for 'universality' (more on that next week), the physicists' belief that something deep and structural ties distant systems together, is the reason network science exists as a field, and the reason a single course can be about friendship, Wikipedia, epidemics and power grids at once. (The textbook's own version of this origin story (Chapter 1, Introduction), and its case that networks are the way into complexity, and complexity the great scientific problem of our time, can be found in the Goodies at the bottom of the page.)

2 · Nodes, edges, and all that

Ok. Now let's define the key terms. A network is a set of nodes (we also sometimes call them vertices, actors, sites) and a set of edges (links, ties, connections) between pairs of them. Sometimes the nodes and edges encode more information.

Here are some important definitions in a slightly messy list:

Degree: A node's degree is the number of edges that connect to it.

Edges: Undirected edges don't have a direction. They are mutual by construction — a Facebook friendship, a co-authorship, a physical cable. Directed edges have a direction: following on Instagram, citing a paper, one Wikipedia page linking to another. The distinction might seem small, but it completely changes how we think about the network. It changes how flows can unfold, and many other aspects. Starting with the simplest example, in a directed network every node has two degrees — how many edges come in and how many go out — and the two can tell completely different stories. (As we will see below, a page everyone links to is famous; a page that links to everyone is a list.) Weighted edges say something about the strength of connections. How many emails, how strong a friendship, how much traffic. Unweighted edges just exist. Most analyses start unweighted and add weights if and when the question demands them.

Networks. Networks inherit these names, so when we talk about undirected networks, unweighted networks, weighted networks, and so on, we just mean networks with those kinds of edges. And there's one more distinction. When we collect data in the wild, weird things can happen. Nodes that link to themselves, more than one link between two nodes, etc. That's also difficult to deal with mathematically, so someone invented simple networks. That's where we allow only one edge per pair of nodes and no self-loops. Simple, unweighted, undirected networks are the default in this course... and in most of network science. Read more about this in Atlas Ch. 7, if you're curious now.

Now for more important definitions.

If you add up all the degrees in an undirected network you get exactly twice the number of edges (because every edge has two ends). It might sound trivial, but if you think about it, we just did some math. Because when you divide by the number of nodes you get the average degree (in a network with n nodes and m edges). Pro tip: This is the kind of common-sense math I might ask about on the pen and paper exam.

And how many edges could there be in a network? We can calculate that too. With n nodes there are n(n-1)/2 possible pairs. If we divide the actual number of edges by the number of possible edges, we get the network's density:. And there's a key empirical finding related to the density. It's almost always really low. This is because real networks tend to be sparse. Many of the networks you will encounter, be they social, neural, or technological, have only a tiny fraction of their possible edges, and almost everything interesting about network structure lives in which few edges made the cut.

Your intuitive mental image might be a picture of nodes connected in some cool way. But how do we write down the networks so other people or computers can analyze them? The simplest way to store a network is the edge list. True to its name, the edge list simply lists the edges one at a time (",,...").

Another common way of representing a network is the adjacency matrix. It contains the same information as the edge list, but in matrix form. The matrix form is convenient for many calculations. We have one row and one column per node, with the entry recording the edge from node i to node j. We put a 0 where there's no connection. If the network is weighted and directed, we place the weight w of the connection between i and j as. And if the network is unweighted and undirected, we use a 1 for each edge, and undirected means symmetric:. In that way, every type of network from above (undirected, directed, weighted, etc.) becomes a visible property of the matrix. The simple-network constraint implies that the diagonal is all zeros (no self-loops).

To get a little sense of why the adjacency matrix is useful, notice that computing the sum across row i gives you node i 's degree (out-degree, if directed); sum down column j, its in-degree. But reading is boring, and you will get a lot more intuition by playing with an actual adjacency matrix. So I made a system for you where you can do just that:

Type numbers into the matrix and watch the network obey — an entry is an edge, its size is the weight, and the row and column sums (the degrees) are computed for you at the margins. Make the matrix symmetric (or press symmetrize) and the arrows lose their heads: an undirected network is a symmetric matrix. Node H starts with an empty row and an empty column — that is what an isolate looks like in matrix form.

3 · Choosing the representation

So we've just been talking about nodes, edges and about how networks are a great way to represent real world systems. But there's one thing I swept under the rug in saying so. Before we even have a network to think about, someone decides what a node is and what an edge is. And that decision determines every conclusion downstream. And it's not always clear what the right network is.

Consider "the network of science." Model it as papers citing papers: nodes live forever, edges point backward in time, and the hubs are classic scientific papers. Model it as scientists co-authoring papers as a team: nodes are active for some period, edges are mutual, and the hubs are sociable lab heads. Same underlying reality, two networks that disagree about almost every question you could ask: who matters, what spreads, what a community is. Neither representation is wrong. Each is a way of representing the same system, but designed to answer different questions.

This is the first skill we test on paper and the first question we will ask about your project: not "what did you measure?" but "why is this the network you measured it on?"

5 · Degree distributions

Read

Network Atlas, Ch. 9 Degree. Degrees and degree distribution are super important in network science. I'll explain why in a bit, but let's just get into it.

One node's degree is a number. All the degrees together form a revealing portrait of the network: the degree distribution. It shows, for each k, how many nodes have exactly k connections. Plot it for many real-world networks and you meet a shape that launched a thousand papers (literally!).

It turns out that many real networks have 'heavy-tailed' distributions. Extreme degree counts are much more likely than you would expect. Take social networks. Most people have only a few links, but then there is a long tail of ever-rarer, ever-better-connected figures stretching far to the right.

On ordinary (linear) axes that tail is not easy to see. Those extreme counts are still rare. But if you plot your degree distribution on log–log axes, then you can see those extremely connected nodes clearly. Whether that tail is "a power law" is a discussion we'll get into next week; this week your job is smaller and more important: make the two plots, and learn to read them. In network science we love distributions.

So let's learn how to plot distributions. Nowadays, there's no need to actually make the plots. Your coding agent can write the matplotlib lines. But you need to learn to read the plots and decide whether to believe the answer (exercise 1.6 is about that). What stays human is reading the shapes.

That is what we will be training here, first in an explorable with two clean mathematical specimens, then on the real thing:

Flip one axis at a time and find the pair that makes each curve straight — that pair is the distribution's signature. Start linear–linear and notice how little you can see.

Intermezzo: Marvel superheroes on Wikipedia. In the following I thought it would be fun to have some real data to work on. So I went to Wikipedia, found a list of all the Marvel characters (the nodes) and then I took all the internal wiki-links that connect them (the edges).

Let's characterize this network with the tools so far. The network has n = 303 nodes. The edges are directed — page A linking to page B says nothing about whether B links back. And there are directed links. Every arrow has one tail and one head, so the average out-degree and the average in-degree are the same number:. Make the network undirected (and ask only which pairs are connected at all) and the directed links collapse to pairs (350 of them link both ways). That gives the undirected average degree. Density: with n = 303 there are possible pairs, of which 1,434 exist, so the density is. About 3%. So 97% of the connections that could exist, don't exist.

By real-world standards this is actually a dense network; many networks you will encounter have a density well below 1%. Finally, 17 characters have no links at all (the isolates). There is one connected blob of 277 characters (91% of the network; we call this the giant connected component — defined properly below) that holds almost everyone else. In between there is a single island of 9 characters. Every number in this paragraph follows from n, m and the edge list alone. Which is exactly why the pen-and-paper test likes this kind of question.

Ok. Intermezzo complete. Now we can use the real data in the explorable. Below you can see the Marvel network's degree distribution, one dot per value of k. I am going to describe the explorable below, but TBH I find it really difficult. Just go play with it and you'll learn more than reading my text. But I'll try anyway.

Start with this setting: In degree only. Linear axes. Raw data. That shows you how many pages have k incoming links. Turn on binning and nothing much happens.

Next: In degree only. Loglog axes. Raw data. Now you see the power-law (or at least something power-law-ish). Turn on binning and you extend the power law.

Now do the same for the out-degree. Observe what happens.

Finally do the same for the undirected.

I am passionate about Binning. For power laws, we often average over ranges of k that widen as k grows. It is a way to make sense of the noise out in that tail of the distribution. This is what happens when you turn binning on: at low k the binned curve sits exactly on the raw dots (there is nothing to average where every value is well populated), and only starts smoothing where the data thins.

One important note is printed on the axis itself: it shows k+1, not k. That is because a log axis can't show zero, and 17 characters have no links at all; the shift keeps them in the picture. Every binning choice in this plot is deliberate, and a note in the Goodies at the bottom of the page explains all of them.

Switch in- to out-degree and watch the leaderboard change characters entirely; then go log–log and turn on binning to see the tail through the noise. Both axis modes plot k+1, so the zero-degree characters survive the log axis instead of silently vanishing.

1.4 — Reading the Marvel degree distributions Learn

Play with the explorable until you can answer these in your own words. It is fine to discuss your answers with a chatbot afterwards. But try to figure them out yourself first — that is the whole point of Part 0. Your weak human brain needs the exercise!

Only the in-degree looks like a power law. On log–log axes the in-degree tail stretches all the way to Spider-Man at k = 106; the out-degree tail dies almost four times earlier — the highest out-degree is Betsy Braddock's, at 28. Why? Think about who creates each kind of edge. An in-link is conferred by someone else: a different article's editors deciding this character matters. An out-link is authored: someone has to sit down and write it into this page. Which of the two has a ceiling, and which can keep growing? (Week 2 has a model of exactly that mechanism.) So what does a node's in-degree mean in this network, and what does its out-degree mean?

Binning extends the power law. With raw data the straight line seems to stop somewhere around; switch binning on and it continues far to the right. Where did the extra range come from: was it hiding, or did the binning invent it? Hint: out in the tail every count is 0 or 1. There is a single character at k = 106 and nobody at 105 or 107... and a log axis can't even show the zeros.

What can go wrong with binning? I am obsessed with getting it right for a reason. Scroll down the binning note in the Goodies and name at least two ways a careless binning could manufacture a power law that is not there — or destroy one that is. (Things to think about: what if you forget to divide by the bin width? What if you bin where the data is dense? Where should a wide bin's dot sit on a log axis? Should you ever fit a slope to a binned plot?) Then the subtle one: degrees are integers. Drop log-spaced bin edges onto integers and what can happen — can one bin hold two integers while its neighbor holds none? And what can go wrong with the very first and the very last bin?

The undirected distribution is the least useful of the three. Switch to it and look at both ends. It counts each neighbor once, whichever way the links point — so it is roughly in-degree plus out-degree, and two different generating mechanisms are mixed into one curve. What does that do to the low end — can an obscure character with zero in-links have an undirected degree of zero? And what does the tail really show you? Explain why a network scientist handed this network would look at in- and out-degree separately.

Ok. I'm dropping in two extra network science vocabulary items so you have them. A path is a sequence of edges from one node to another; the distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path; and a connected component is a set of nodes that can all reach each other via a path (it's a bit more complicated for directed graphs, check the book for that). Many real networks have a single giant component connecting nearly every node, plus a few scattered islands — just like the superheroes do. The deeper theory of paths (and the famous "six degrees") arrives in week 3.

6 · Seeing a network

Data visualization is a crucial part of network science. Networks are often beautiful. And there's lots to learn about plotting graphs. Today we get started and scratch the surface.

The first thing I do with a network is to plot it. The force-directed layout (NetworkX's default) pulls connected nodes together and pushes nodes in general apart, and for a few hundred nodes it is genuinely revealing: clusters, hubs and loners jump out. But know what you are looking at. Node positions in a force layout are a good start. They tell you what to look for. But in the end the node locations depend on a physics simulation (there's an energy function that is optimized) so you always have to be critical. Very large networks are difficult to visualize: maybe you run out of pixels on the screen, or you don't have enough compute to calculate a good layout. And if the networks are very dense you'll get a big undifferentiated hairball. In short, there are many ways network visualization can go wrong.

For now, let's look at the superheroes and enjoy the many ways we can organize the nodes.

To make the visualizations more fun, I have calculated the network communities. This is something we'll be getting into during week 4. But for now the idea is simply that we can find groups of nodes that are more connected than what we would expect if things were wired up randomly... kind of like groups of friends in a social network. I've given each community a color and created a visualization that organizes according to community.

Flip between layouts and watch the same 277 characters (the giant component) fly to new homes — the edges never change, only the story the picture tells. Toggle the community colors to see the groups a clustering algorithm finds (week 4 explains how); in the community circle, edges bend toward the center so each cluster's internal chatter hugs the rim. Then hover a hub (go find Spider-Man) and watch one neighborhood survive every rearrangement.

Essentials

This is the Learn core of the week, in one place: the definitions you must know cold, and what you should be able to do with them on paper. If a term below feels shaky, the section that teaches it is one click up the page — and if you can do everything here with a pen, you are ready for this week's share of Test 1.

Network

nodes (the objects) connected by edges (their relationships). We write n for the number of nodes and m for the number of edges.

Directed · undirected

directed edges point — says nothing about; undirected edges simply connect the pair. Wikipedia links are directed.

Weighted · unweighted

a weighted edge carries a number (intensity, capacity, a count); an unweighted edge just exists.

Simple network

at most one edge per pair of nodes, no self-loops. The default in this course — and in most of network science.

Degree

the number of edges connected to node i; directed networks split it into in-degree and out-degree. Summing all degrees gives exactly 2m, because every edge has two ends.

Average degree

Density

the fraction of possible edges that actually exist:. Real networks are almost always sparse — the interesting structure lives in which few edges made the cut.

Edge list

the network written down as a list of its edges, one pair at a time.

Adjacency matrix

one row and one column per node; entry records the edge from i to j (its weight, or 1 / 0). Undirected symmetric:.

Path · distance

a path is a sequence of edges leading from one node to another; the distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path between them.

Connected component

a set of nodes that can all reach each other. An isolate is a node with no edges at all; the giant component is the one component that holds most of the network.

Degree distribution

for each value of k, how many nodes have exactly k connections. The single most revealing portrait of a network.

Heavy tail

extreme degrees are far more common than a bell curve would allow. Nearly invisible on linear axes — plot on log–log axes to see it.

Binning

averaging a noisy distribution over ranges of k that widen as k grows. Done right, the binned curve sits exactly on the raw data where the data is dense; our plots show k+1 so the degree-0 nodes survive the log axis.

On the first test

From this week, you should be able to do the following on paper, closed book. Any formula you need is printed on the test paper — you are never asked to write one from memory:

translate a described real-world system into nodes and edges — and defend the choice, or say what a different choice would change;

classify a network from its description: directed / undirected, weighted / unweighted — and spot when a representation is wrong for the question being asked of it;

compute, on a ≤6-node example: degrees, in- and out-degrees, edge counts, average degree and density — and use the fact that degrees sum to twice the edge count;

reconcile the directed and undirected views of the same network: explain why the directed link count and the undirected pair count differ, why the average in- and out-degree must be equal, and what happens to the isolates if a careless loading drops them;

write the adjacency matrix of a small network — directed or undirected, weighted or not — and read a matrix back into a drawing; know why undirected means symmetric, and where the degrees hide in the rows and columns;

sketch or read a degree distribution — including knowing what changes between linear and log–log axes, why heavy tails vanish on the former, and what a heavy in-degree tail next to a short out-degree tail says about how each kind of edge comes to exist;

say what binning does to a noisy degree distribution — where the binned curve must sit exactly on the raw data, why the plots show k+1 rather than k, and at least two ways a careless binning can manufacture a power law that is not there (or destroy one that is);

use the definitions of path, distance, and connected component on a small example;

look at a drawing of a network and say what in it is data and what is layout — and name one conclusion the drawing invites that the data does not support;

read a short network description or figure and identify what is a node property, what is an edge property, and what is a property of the whole network.

Networks in the context of science. Network Atlas, Ch. 1 Introduction — the textbook's own origin story, and its opening argument (its section 1.1 is literally titled "Why Complexity is the Key"): that complex systems are the great unsolved scientific problem of our time, and networks are the way in. Read it if you want to know where this field sits in science at large, not just what it computes.

A second textbook, straight from the source. Last year this course ran on Network Science by Albert-László Barabási — one of the field's founders, free online, and a genuinely different take: physics-flavored, model-first, with the scale-free story told by the person who started it. Ch. 1 and Ch. 2 cover this week's ground. Where the Atlas is broad and encyclopedic, Barabási is a narrative — read the two side by side and you learn as much from where they disagree as from where they agree.

Six degrees, tested on the internet. Veritasium's The Science of Six Degrees of Separation (2015) is the video that put the small-world idea in front of millions — Milgram's original letter experiment, the Kevin Bacon game, and a live replication where viewers tried to reach Derek Muller by forwarding an email through people they knew. Average chain length: about three. Week 3 is where we do the mathematics of why.

An explorable in the spirit of this page, but longer. Nicky Case's The Wisdom and/or Madness of Crowds is a playable essay about networks and people: why the crowd around you can look nothing like the population (the majority illusion), how small worlds arise, and when ideas and behaviors spread — or don't — depending only on the wiring. Half an hour, no equations, and it quietly covers weeks 1 through 3 before we get to them.

The hidden influence of social networks. Nicholas Christakis's TED talk (2010, 19 minutes) on the Framingham study: obesity, smoking, happiness and loneliness all clustering in a real social network — and the hard question of whether that is contagion, homophily, or something else. It is the sociological argument for this course in one talk.

For the one student who cares about binning

You noticed the axis says k+1 and the button says "binned," and you want to know exactly what was done to the data. Good instinct. Here is the full confession.

Why bin at all. In a heavy-tailed distribution the tail is made of rare events — up there, every count is 0 or 1, and raw P(k) degenerates into a jagged scatter (one dot per lonely character). Binning trades resolution for statistical reliability: average neighboring degrees together and the trend comes out of the noise. But binning where the data is dense buys no reliability and just blurs your best measurements.

So the scheme is mixed. Working in u = k+1: bins of width 1 for, then doubling bins,,,. Each bin's count is divided by the bin's width — otherwise a bin would look popular merely for being wide — so what is plotted is a probability density per unit of degree. That normalization (over all 303 nodes, isolates included) is why the width-1 bins coincide exactly with raw P(k): where the bins are width 1, "binned" and "raw" are the same number, and the curve only becomes an abstraction where it has to. Each doubling bin is drawn at the geometric mean of the integers it covers — sits at — the midpoint as a log axis sees it, not the arithmetic middle, which would sit visibly right of center.

The k+1 shift. A log axis cannot show zero, and this network has 17 characters with no links at all — plot raw k and they silently vanish, which is exactly the kind of quiet data loss this course wants you allergic to. Shifting to k+1 keeps them in the picture at the cost of slightly bending the low end of the curve (k = 0, 1, 2 move the most; the tail barely notices). The rule, if you ever do this yourself: the axis label must say so. Ours does.

Integers are not a continuum. Degrees are integers, and the standard recipe — np.logspace for the bin edges, then a histogram — quietly assumes they are not. Near the low end, log-spaced edges fall between consecutive integers: with edges at 1, 1.3, 1.8, 2.4, 3.2,... some bins contain no integer at all (they plot as zero, or on a log axis simply vanish) while a neighbor swallows two integers and looks twice as populated as it should. Dividing by the nominal bin width does not repair this; you have to divide by the number of integers the bin actually contains — or make the edges integers in the first place. The ends need the same care. Round fractional edges to integers and you can get two identical edges: a zero-width bin, or the first two bins silently merged into one. At the top, the largest degree sits exactly on the last edge and, depending on the library's half-open convention, either drops out of the histogram or lands in a bin that has quietly merged with its neighbor. This is why the scheme above uses width-1 bins where the degrees are dense and integer edges (powers of two) after that: every bin holds a known set of integers, none is empty by construction, and the normalization is honest.

What binning is not for. Reading shapes, yes. Fitting exponents, no — never fit "the slope of a power law" to shifted, binned data (or, honestly, to a raw log–log plot either). The grown-up tool needs no bins at all: the cumulative distribution. It finds you in week 2.
