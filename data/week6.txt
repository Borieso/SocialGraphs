A quick status. You have now learned to represent language with numbers using tokens, counts and document-term matrices. These tools turned language into something a computer can store and compare. But we ended on two problems.
The first one you saw in the lookalikes explorable. With raw counts, the, and and of dominated every comparison, and the pages only started to find their real neighbors once we threw a stopword list at them by hand. The second was the dog, puppy and democracy example. When every word has its own coordinate, every word is exactly equally far from every other word. A count vector does not know what a word means, and a Bag-of-Words representation can miss that two documents are expressing almost the same underlying concept... simply because they use different words.
One way to get closer to meaning is to look at context. Words that occur in similar surroundings often have something in common, and documents that use language in similar ways may also be related even when they do not contain exactly the same words.
This week we use that idea at two levels. First, we represent entire documents and compare which pages are similar, which terms distinguish groups of pages, and which themes recur across a corpus. Then we change the object and represent individual words through the contexts in which they occur.
A useful way to remember the split is that documents are represented by their words; later, words are represented by their contexts.

1 · Documents need better weights
Raw word counts reward frequent words. That sounds reasonable until the same boring words are frequent everywhere. A term can appear many times on one page and still tell us very little about what makes that page distinctive (if the same term also appears on almost every other page).
That was exactly what we saw in the lookalikes explorable, and removing stopwords by hand was a blunt fix: somebody has to write the list, and it only knows about English, not about Marvel.
TF-IDF tackles the same problem, but using the data itself. It combines two pieces of information: how frequent a term is within each document, and how unusual that term is relative to the full collection of documents.
The TF-IDF idea comes from information retrieval. Imagine searching for the superpower. A document should not rank highly simply because it contains the hundreds of times. The word superpower is much more informative because it occurs in fewer documents. TF-IDF therefore gives a term a high weight when it occurs often in a particular document, but discounts it when it also occurs in many other documents. (If you built last week's 20-line search engine, this is the fix it was missing.)

Cosine similarity, a second look. You met cosine similarity last week in the lookalikes explorable. Now that the vectors are full of weights rather than counts, it is worth slowing down to see why cosine similarity is the right way to compare them.
One option would be ordinary Euclidean distance, but that is awkward for text because the length of a document can dominate the comparison. A long article and a short paragraph about the same thing can end up far apart simply because one contains more words.
Cosine similarity compares the direction of two vectors. For vectors and,
The dot product is large when the same dimensions contain large values in both vectors. Dividing by the lengths of the two vectors removes the effect of their overall magnitude, so what remains depends only on the angle between them. This is why multiplying a document vector by 10 does not change its cosine similarity with any other vector. Its length changes, but its direction does not. Two vectors pointing in exactly the same direction have cosine similarity 1. For the non-negative TF-IDF vectors we use here, the cosine similarity is never below 0, and vectors with no weighted vocabulary in common have similarity 0.

2 · Compare groups of documents
The network part of the course gave us structural communities without reading a single page. Text lets us ask what those communities are actually talking about.
"Scattertext" is a tool for comparing two labeled collections of text term-by-term. It counts how often words or phrases are used in each corpus and allows the user to see shared and distinctive language at the same time. The original Scattertext package and examples contain several ways of scoring and displaying those differences.

3 · Find recurring themes
TF-IDF and Scattertext describe documents mainly through individual terms. Sometimes several different words are part of a broader recurring pattern. election, voter, parliament, minister and government, for example, can all contribute to a linguistic theme we might interpret as politics even if no single politics-related document contains all of them.
Topic modeling is what we call a large family of methods designed to find patterns of words that belong together in a collection of documents. The basic goal of topic modeling is to move away from the dependence on individual words and instead find recurring groups of related words: words that tend to appear in the same documents.
These topics (groups of related words) can help us summarize what's going on in a large corpus, compare documents, or ask whether different parts of the corpus tend to discuss different things.
Read (Optional)
Skim David Blei's classic Probabilistic Topic Models. Read it for the conceptual picture: recurring topics are distributions over words, and documents can contain several topics at once. You do not need the inference machinery for this course, so only dig into that part if you're interested.
There are many different topic-modeling methods. They all have strengths and weaknesses, and different methods define a "topic" in different ways. Some work directly on the document-term matrix: non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), for example, splits it into a small set of word groups and records how strongly each document uses each group. Newer methods start from document embeddings instead, cluster similar documents, and then describe each cluster by its most characteristic words; next week's BERTopic is one of these. Here we go deeper with Latent Dirichlet Allocation, LDA, because it keeps two things especially clear: what a topic is, and what a document is made of.
In LDA, a topic is a probability distribution over words, and a document is a mixture of topics. Imagine a topic whose high-probability words are mutant, school, gene, telepath and sentinel. We might decide to call it mutants, but that label comes from us. The model only gives us the probabilities. A particular document might then be 80% of that topic and 20% of another topic.
LDA is a generative model: it tells a step-by-step story of how the documents could have been produced. The generative story behind LDA is useful in order to understand what the model assumes. For every word position in a document, we basically imagine that we first choose a topic according to that document's topic mixture and then choose a word according to that topic's word distribution. In real data we only observe the words. LDA works backwards and estimates the hidden topic-word distributions and document-topic mixtures that could plausibly have produced them.
The two levels are easy to mix up at first, so the explorable below puts them next to each other. Choose a document and first look at its mixture across topics. Those probabilities tell you how strongly each topic contributes to that document. Then select one of the topics and inspect its distribution over words. Those probabilities tell you which words are most characteristic of the topic. Keep track of what each distribution sums over: topics for a document, or words for a topic.
Change the document and read both levels of the model: first the document's mixture over topics, then the selected topic's distribution over words. Remember that the topic names are human interpretations of the word distributions.
This also means that topics are not facts waiting inside the corpus to be discovered. You choose how many topics the model should look for (this is called model selection and for my money one of the hardest things in the universe, so don't spend too much time on it). And preprocessing, random initialization and the corpus itself can all affect the result. A list of top words is therefore only the beginning of the interpretation. You should also inspect the documents with high weight on that topic before you decide what it means.

4 · Now let's think about words
So far the rows we cared about represented documents. Now the question changes. How can a vector represent the meaning of a word?
Last week's answer was disappointing. In last week's one-hot vectors a word gets its own coordinate and nothing else, so dog is exactly as close to puppy as it is to democracy: cosine 0 in both cases. To do better, a word's vector has to be built from something other than its identity.
One way to make the intuition visible is to give you a word you may not know and let the surrounding sentences do the work. Take tezgüino.
Read these four sentences
A bottle of tezgüino sits on the table.
Everybody likes tezgüino.
Tezgüino makes you drunk.
We make tezgüino from corn.
I think you have a pretty good feel for tezgüino from these examples. Pretty cool, right?
This is a classic example in distributional semantics. Lin (1998) uses a slightly modified version of an example from Nida (1975, p. 167) to show how the meaning of an unfamiliar word can be inferred from context. Nobody gave you a dictionary definition here, but you inferred that tezgüino is some kind of alcoholic drink from the company the word keeps.
That is the intuition behind the distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. J. R. Firth's famous formulation is "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."
Read
Jurafsky & Martin, Chapter 5, Embeddings, sections 5.1–5.4. Read for the distributional hypothesis, count-based word vectors and cosine similarity. Lena Voita's Word Embeddings notes are a useful visual companion.
To turn the intuition into cold, hard math, choose a context window around every occurrence of a target word and count the neighboring words. Stack those rows and you have a word-context matrix. In that matrix each row is a word, while each column records how often a particular context word appeared near it.

Importantly, the size of the context window is part of the model. A narrow window pays more attention to immediate linguistic context. A wider window tends to pick up broader topical associations. Two words with similar rows appeared in similar surroundings, and we can once again use our old friend, the cosine similarity, to compare them.
The object has changed, but the recipe should feel familiar: define the dimensions, build a vector for each object, then compare the vectors.

5 · Raw co-occurrence is not enough
Raw co-occurrence has the same problem as raw document counts. A word such as the occurs near almost everything. Pointwise mutual information, PMI, asks whether a word-context pair occurs more often than we would expect, if the two were independent:
PMI is conventionally written with a base-2 logarithm because of the connection to information theory (see also Jurafsky & Martin and the explorable below). But the base isn't really important. As is the case with IDF, the base only rescales the values: zero stays zero and the signs do not change.
If and occur together exactly as often as independence would predict, the ratio in the PMI equation is 1 and PMI is 0. A positive value means the pair occurs together more often than expected. A negative value means it occurs together less often than expected. If you start from a word-context count matrix, you can estimate these probabilities from the counts: the cell gives the joint count for and, the row and column totals give the marginal counts, and the sum of the whole matrix gives the total number of counted word-context pairs.
Positive PMI, PPMI, keeps the positive values and replaces negative ones with zero:
When we use PPMI, common but uninformative contexts lose influence, while unexpectedly strong associations stand out.

6 · Learn dense word embeddings
OK. So I was just proudly exclaiming that we'd gotten to embeddings in a simple way. But now we're getting to the juicy part. We're going to learn about and play around with the word embedding that changed the world and in many ways kicked off the impressive new era of language technology that we're currently living through.
Count-based word vectors are high-dimensional and sparse because every possible context word is a dimension. Word2Vec takes a different route and learns much shorter, dense vectors without explicitly keeping that whole matrix.
The basic trick is to turn context into a prediction task. I'm not going to give you the whole explanation here, Jurafsky and Martin do it better. But I'll tell you the two key terms to keep an eye out for:
Skip-gram gives the model a center word and train it to recognize words that occur nearby.
CBOW runs the prediction in the other direction: the surrounding words are used to predict the center word.
As this happens again and again across a corpus, words that are useful in similar contexts tend to end up with similar vectors.
Negative sampling makes the training manageable. Instead of comparing an observed word-context pair with every word in a large vocabulary, the model compares it to a small number of sampled words that did not occur in that context. That is the intuition you need here; the reading below goes further into how the vectors are actually learned.

GloVe is another static embedding method. It learns dense vectors from global word co-occurrence statistics rather than the local prediction task used by Word2Vec. For this course, the idea it shares with Word2Vec is most important: both methods place words in a learned vector space where distance and direction can carry regularities in how words are used.
It's important to realize that count-based and prediction-based embeddings are closer than they first appear. Skip-gram with negative sampling has a mathematical connection to a shifted PMI matrix. You do not need that derivation for Test 2 (the curious will find it in the Goodies). The broader point is enough: counting contexts and predicting contexts can recover related structure from the same corpus. We once again compare the dense vectors from Word2Vec and GloVe using cosine similarity.
A famous intuition-building example. Aligned vectors often correspond to related words, and some relations can even appear as directions in the space.
One famous example is
king - man + woman ≈ queen
In some trained static embedding spaces, the direction from man to king is similar to the direction from woman to queen. If we start at the vector for king, subtract the vector for man, and add the vector for woman, the resulting point can therefore land close to queen. The example is memorable because it turns an abstract vector space into something we can reason about geometrically. It does not mean that every analogy works, or that the model has learned a neat human definition of royalty or gender.

Bias is always there. Static embeddings also learn associations we may not want. If occupations or social groups occur in stereotyped contexts, the vectors can encode those regularities too. The map above shows it: color it by gender and switch to the war ↔ home axis, and the home end is almost all women, pulled there mostly by the words her and she. GloVe has learned that female pronouns sit closer to family and love than to soldier and mission. A single static vector also has trouble with polysemy. bank gets one location whether the sentence is about money or a river.

This week started with documents and ended with words. In both cases we turned patterns in text into vectors and used geometry to compare them, and in both cases the vectors were blind to one thing: context inside the sentence. Every bank got the same vector, river or money.
Next week the vectors become contextual, and whole texts get a single embedding that captures more than their word counts. Then we put the representations to work as measurement tools: walking through a corpus by meaning, measuring sentiment, turning names in prose into structured data, and searching by meaning rather than by keyword. Essentials
This is the Learn core of the week in one place. The recurring question is simple: what does one vector represent, what do its dimensions mean, and what information was used to place two vectors close together? If you can answer that and do the small calculations below with a pen, you are ready for this week's share of Test 2.
TF-IDF
a document-term weighting that combines how frequent a term is inside one document with how rare it is across the collection. Terms that occur everywhere are downweighted.
Term frequency, TF
the frequency of term inside document; here.
Document frequency, DF
the number of documents in the corpus that contain a term at least once.
Inverse document frequency, IDF
a weight that increases as a term appears in fewer documents; here.
Cosine similarity
the normalized dot product. It compares vector direction rather than overall length.
Scattertext
a tool for comparing two labeled corpora term by term, placing words or phrases according to how they are used in the two groups so shared and distinctive language can be inspected together.
Topic model
a family of methods for finding recurring patterns of word use across a collection of documents. Different topic models define the latent structure differently.
LDA
Latent Dirichlet Allocation, a probabilistic topic model in which each topic is a distribution over words and each document is a mixture over topics.
Distributional hypothesis
words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings or uses.
Word-context matrix
rows are target words, columns are context words, and cells record how often the context word occurs near the target under a chosen window definition.
PMI
pointwise mutual information,, comparing observed word-context co-occurrence with what independence would predict.
PPMI
positive PMI,; negative associations are set to zero.
Static word embedding
one vector per word type, regardless of the sentence in which it occurs. PPMI rows, Word2Vec and GloVe are different ways of constructing such vectors.
Skip-gram
a Word2Vec training setup where a center word is used to predict nearby context words.
CBOW
continuous bag of words, the reverse Word2Vec setup: nearby context words are used to predict the center word.
Negative sampling
a cheaper training objective that contrasts observed word-context pairs with a small sample of unobserved pairs rather than scoring the entire vocabulary every time.
GloVe
a dense static embedding method learned from global word co-occurrence statistics.

Why scikit-learn's TF-IDF disagrees with this page
Spoiler for exercise 6.2, so finish it first. TfidfVectorizer makes three choices that differ from our definitions. Its term frequency is the raw count, not the count divided by document length. Its IDF is smoothed,, as if one extra document contained every term once, which guards against dividing by zero for a term that was never seen. And it rescales every document vector to length 1. Two of those three do not matter for cosine at all, because cosine ignores length anyway. The one that does matter is the final: it exists so that a term found in every document is "not entirely ignored," in scikit-learn's own words, and it is why the keeps a weight of 1. Turning the smoothing off with smooth_idf=False moves D1–D3 from 0.139 to 0.102, but no setting removes the, so to reproduce this page exactly you subtract it yourself from idf_. There are dozens of TF-IDF variants in use; none is the TF-IDF, which is why a paper should always say which one it used.
Counting and predicting are secretly the same
Section 6 says that Skip-gram with negative sampling is connected to a shifted PMI matrix. Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg's Neural Word Embedding as Implicit Matrix Factorization (2014) makes that precise: at its optimum, the dot product of a word vector and a context vector equals the PMI of the pair minus, where is the number of negative samples. So Word2Vec is, in effect, compressing a shifted version of the PMI matrix from Section 5 into a few hundred dense dimensions. It is a short and readable paper, and a lovely example of an idea that looked new turning out to be an old idea in disguise.
A visual route into embeddings
Lena Voita's Word Embeddings notes are one of the nicest visual explanations of distributional meaning, count-based vectors, Word2Vec, GloVe and the choices hidden in context windows. If one of today's explanations felt abstract, start here.
Scattertext beyond the teaching view
The Scattertext repository and examples show how much further you can take the two-corpus comparison: alternative term scores, phrases, interactive inspection and different ways of comparing categories. It is also a useful source of code when you build your own version.
Dialectograms: when two communities use the same word differently
Scattertext finds the words one community uses more than another. A harder question is how two communities use the same word: freedom, say, or police. In Analyzing differences between discursive communities using dialectograms (Enggaard, Lohse, Pedersen and Lehmann, Scientific Reports, 2024; preprint) we answer it with tools from this very page. We train a separate GloVe embedding on each community's text, pick one focal word, and plot the vocabulary on two axes, one per community, according to how strongly each community's embedding ties a word to the focal word. Like Scattertext, the plot puts shared and characteristic language side by side, but now for one word's meaning rather than for word counts. We also introduce a measure, sense separation, built on the PMI from Section 5, that ranks which words the two communities use most differently without simply surfacing rare words or words with many meanings. We applied it to comments from two US political subreddits, one Democratic and one Republican; the code is on GitHub if you want to try it on two Marvel communities.
