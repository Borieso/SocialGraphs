1 · Who matters?
Ask most people who have looked at the Marvel network for five minutes who the most important character is and there is a good chance that they will say Spider-Man.
He has the most links (106 characters link to his page), and by every measure on this page he comes out on top. Fine. Now ask the second question: who is number five? Or: which character would you remove to split the Marvel universe into pieces? Or: whose page should a new character try to get a link from? Each of those is a question about importance, and each one has a different answer.
This week we will be talking about centrality. From its roots in sociology, network science has dozens of centrality measures, and a useful way to think about them is as a small toolbox where each tool answers one question well:
Degree centrality: how many direct connections do you have?
Closeness centrality: how quickly can you reach everyone?
Betweenness centrality: how often are you the bridge others must cross?
Eigenvector centrality / PageRank: are you connected to people who are themselves important?
The first three measures are based on paths, so that is where we start. The last one is built out of a random walk, which is a different and beautiful idea (and it is also the single idea that made Google Google).

2 · Paths, distances, and the machine that computes them
Let's begin with some definitions that are interesting in and of themselves, but also lay the foundation for what we will be learning in the next few parts.
A walk is any sequence of nodes where each consecutive pair is linked. A path is a walk that never repeats a node. The distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path between them, and if no path exists the distance is infinite. Last week you actually used three numbers built from distances: the average path length (over all pairs in the giant component), the diameter (the longest shortest path), and the small-world fact that both grow like.
The trick to calculating all these paths is breadth-first search (BFS). Stand on a node. Everyone it links to is at distance 1. Everyone they link to that you have not seen yet is at distance 2. Repeat until nothing new appears. The nodes come out in rings, and the ring a node lands in is its distance. One BFS gives you the distance from one node to every other. If you run it from every node you have all the distances,, the diameter, and everything else on this page that involves a path.
Start from Spider-Man and press Step: ring 1 is his 106 direct links, ring 2 adds 152 more, and ring 3 finishes the network. Nobody in the giant component is more than three steps from Spider-Man; he is the network's center. Now pick a character from the far end of the list (try one you have never heard of), step again, and count the rings. Then switch to Follow arrows out and start from Spider-Man once more. His page links to only nine characters, so the wave crawls, and 46 characters are never reached at all.
Two things to notice about the real network. First, as we know from last week, it's a small world (the distances are short). The Marvel giant component has and diameter 6, and 86% of all pairs of characters are exactly two or three steps apart. Second, direction matters. The network is built from Wikipedia links, and a link from A to B does not mean B links back. If you follow arrows, a pair of nodes can be close one way and far, or unreachable, the other way. Spider-Man is within three steps of everyone when you ignore direction, but from Spider-Man's page you can only reach 230 of the characters.
So connected components are more complicated in directed networks. The largest strongly connected component (every node can reach every other node by following the arrows) contains 229 of the 303 characters. The rest can be reached but cannot get back, or the other way around. When a question is about reaching, spreading, or flowing, it's a good idea to think about whether the arrows matter before you compute your metrics.

3 · The first two answers: degree and closeness
Now we can get started on the actual measures.
Degree centrality is a rebranding of the metric you already know and love:, or in a directed network and separately. It is local (it never looks past a node's own links), it is cheap, and it is often right. In the Marvel network, in-degree answers "who is mentioned most," and out-degree answers "whose page mentions the most others," which are different questions with different "central nodes." The Hulk, for example, is mentioned by 64 pages (second only to Spider-Man) but his page mentions just ten characters; Betsy Braddock is mentioned by seven, yet her page mentions 28, the most of anyone.
Closeness centrality is more complex and draws on the paths from Part 2. It asks how near a given node is to everyone. It's defined by taking the mean distance from to all other nodes and then using the inverse (so that closeness to others corresponds to large values):
So a node with closeness is, on average, two steps from everybody. Spider-Man's is (mean distance); the median character sits at. Another way of putting it is that closeness is a measure of network reach: if you want to spread a rumor, a disease, or an update as fast as possible, a good bet is to start from the node with the highest closeness (there's a whole cottage industry of papers focused on where to start rumors for maximum effect, and closeness isn't the best... but pretty good).

4 · Betweenness: the broker
The Medici story. Let's time-travel. You're in Florence (Firenze for us Europeans), around 1430. At that time, the city was run by a few dozen elite families. They were all connected to each other in complicated ways by marriage. This is the network that the political scientist John Padgett spent years reconstructing. In the resulting marriage network the Medici family was the best connected, but only just. They had six marriage ties, but the other families were also well connected. The Guadagni family had four links and the Strozzi (who were richer) four.
Yet within a few years Cosimo de' Medici controlled the city. The cool thing is that the marriage network can help us understand why. Of the Medici's six partner families, only one pair had a tie to each other. Every other family that wanted to deal with a Medici partner had to go through the Medici. More than half of all shortest paths between the other families run through the Medici. That's twice as many as the share of any rival. So the Medici were only a little better connected than their rivals. What set them apart was that they were the family in between.
The centrality that reveals what is special about the Medici family is betweenness centrality. For every pair of other nodes, count the fraction of their shortest paths that pass through, and add it all up:
where is the number of shortest paths from to and the number of them that go through. (Divide by the number of pairs, for an undirected network, to get a value between 0 and 1, which is what networkx reports.) A node with high betweenness is a broker, a bridge, a bottleneck: remove it and traffic has to reroute, or cannot flow at all. Betweenness is the measure of control.
Notice what it is not: it is not degree. A node can have two links and enormous betweenness if it is the only door to part of the network, and a node can have thirty links and betweenness near zero if all thirty neighbors are also linked to each other. 5 · Who has important friends?
When you consider degree, you're making an assumption that every link is equally important. But if you think about your own life, that's not the case. Some of the people you know are much more important than others. So it's not just about how many people you know, but who you know. In the Marvel network, a link from Spider-Man's page is worth more than a link from a character nobody has heard of. If we define importance by having many links, this starts to sound circular! But as you'll see in a moment, it's circular in a way that will work out nicely and take you back to your linear algebra classes.
Anyway. A node is important if it is linked to by important nodes. As an equation, we can write the importance of node as:
Every node's score is the sum of its neighbors' scores, then rescaled by a constant. A vector that satisfies this for every node at once is an eigenvector of the adjacency matrix. The eigenvector with only positive entries is eigenvector centrality.
We calculate it through iteration: give everyone a score of 1, replace each score by the sum of the neighbors' scores, rescale, repeat until nothing changes. In the kite explorable, that is why D wins: not the most links, but links to the best-linked nodes.
PageRank is the closely related idea in a directed network. (It is also the idea that made Google: in 1998 Larry Page and Sergey Brin pictured a random walker who sits on a page, clicks a random link, and keeps clicking forever. A page's importance, then, is the fraction of time the walk spends there.)
In the random walk, each page sends the walker out among its out-links, so a link from a page that links to few others is worth more than in the classic eigenvector centrality. In network science terms, it means that Page and Brin were estimating the eigenvectors of a slightly different matrix. The walk matrix, we can call it. That matrix is just the adjacency matrix with each row normalized to sum to one.
You can't just take the eigenvector of that matrix and be done, though, and the reason is related to what we discussed in Part 2: in a directed network you can't get everywhere from everywhere. (The same is true on the real web, which is what Page and Brin were trying to index). Pages with no out-links trap the random walker, and groups of pages that only link to each other can trap him too. Page and Brin fixed this using a simple trick. 6 · Compared to what?
Last week's null-model point does not go away because the measures got more advanced.
Spider-Man has the highest betweenness in the Marvel network: 23% of all shortest paths run through him. Is that a finding? Shuffle the network 200 times with the degree-preserving shuffle from week 2 and recompute. In the shuffled networks Spider-Man's betweenness averages 23% as well.
That means that his betweenness is similar to what a node with 106 links will have in any network with this degree distribution. Now look at good old Hercules: 26 links, betweenness, against in the shuffles, four standard deviations out. Or Black Widow: 25 links, twice her shuffled value. Those characters are brokers, characters whose position, not their popularity, connects different parts of the universe (Hercules links the Avengers, the Hulk's circle, the cosmic characters, and Asgard). And Black Cat, with 28 links is decidedly not a broker. She has a third of the betweenness her degree predicts: 24 of her 28 neighbors are also Spider-Man's, so almost every path near her has a shortcut around her.
From an AI-drafted report
"Betweenness centrality identifies Spider-Man as the critical broker of the Marvel universe: 23% of all shortest paths pass through him, far more than any other character. His closeness of 0.59 confirms this, showing he can reach 59% of the network directly."
The quote has three claims, two of them are wrong. Find them before you read on.
(Solution: As we just discussed, the first claim is not a finding, it is driven by his degree, see above. The third claim misreads closeness, which is an inverse mean distance, not a fraction of anything. The middle claim is simply true.)
So I suggest that you try to cultivate two habits for every centrality number you ever report:
Say which question. Degree for popularity, closeness for reach, betweenness for control, PageRank for prestige. On the Marvel network the four top-ten lists overlap on six to eight names, and the rank correlation between degree and betweenness is, so most of the time they agree, and the disagreements are the interesting part.
Say compared to what. This one is more general. It really is something you should try to do for any measure. You can only really understand if a centrality high or low if you compare it to a null model. The degree-preserving shuffle is the right one for the "is this node's position more than its degree?" question.

7 · Mixing patterns: do the important stick together?
Ok. Until now, I've been great at constructing a coherent narrative across these pages, but I can't keep it up any more. In this Part and the next, I'll do quick intros to other important measures that will tell you about network structure. They are also discussed in the video.
Read
Network Atlas, Ch. 31 Quantitative Assortativity, section 31.1 Degree Correlations (31.2 revisits the friendship paradox from this angle). Ch. 30 Homophily, 30.2 Quantifying Homophily, is the same idea for attributes other than degree.
Degree assortativity. Every measure we've looked at today scores a single node. Now ask a question about pairs: when we consider a node's neighbors, does it connect to other hubs, or does it pick the small fry... and how does that depend on the degree of the node itself? Among scientists, the stars co-author with stars, so hubs are densely interconnected. On the Internet, the big backbone routers connect to thousands of tiny ones, so hubs are spread out across the network by design.
One of my favorite network scientists, Mark Newman, calls this a network's mixing pattern. The way to quantify it is via the degree assortativity. This is basically the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link, taken over all links.
When we say that the network is assortative, like links to like, hubs to hubs. When we say the network is disassortative, hubs to low-degree nodes. When, your degree says nothing about your neighbor's. A classic finding is that social networks tend to be assortative (physics co-authorship, film actors) while technological and biological ones tend to be disassortative (the Internet, protein interactions).
Visualizing assortativity. A picture can capture more than the single number. Below we construct a plot where for each degree, we take the nodes with that degree and calculate the average degree of their neighbors:, the average neighbor degree. If that line is rising, the network is assortative, falling means disassortative, and flat means no mixing at all. The flat line sits at, which you met last week as the friendship paradox: it is the expected degree of a random neighbor.
Marvel has. The characters with a single link sit next to a neighbor of degree 45 on average; the ten biggest hubs' neighbors average 19. Mildly disassortative, so a technological network rather than a social one? Before you write that sentence: compared to what. Fifty degree-preserving shuffles give, and the real value is 1.4 standard deviations from that. Marvel's disassortativity is what its degree sequence forces. A node with 106 links in a network of 277 has no choice but to link mostly to small nodes, because there are not enough hubs to go around. This is called structural disassortativity, and every heavy-tailed network has some. The explorable makes the limit visible: rewire toward assortative and the process stalls near with the hubs out of hubs to link to and the network falling into pieces as leaves pair off with leaves; rewire toward disassortative and it reaches, a hub-and-spoke picture with almost no triangles left.
Choose Disassortative and press Play: the hubs spread out, each wrapped in its own halo of leaves, the dots tip downward, and collapses. Reset, choose Assortative, play again: the hubs pull into one dense core, small components break off, and the dots rise until the rewiring runs out of moves. Then try Random, the week-2 shuffle, and watch land near, not. That is the assortativity that comes from network structure alone. A deep dive on all this can be found in the Goodies.
Assortativity, more generally. Degree is one attribute of nodes, but we can generalize to any attribute: age, gender, political leaning, which team a character belongs to. Calculating assortativity for any of these will often tell us more about the mechanisms that determine link-creation in a network (and we can test if our findings are significant by shuffling attributes giving us a new kind of null model, see the Goodies).
One last connection: Assortative mixing by attribute is another name for homophily, one of the oldest findings in the sociology branch of network analysis. Our Marvel network is full of it: X-Men link to X-Men, Avengers to Avengers. The formula from above with categories instead of degrees gives the attribute assortativity (networkx has it as attribute_assortativity_coefficient). 8 · Cliques
One last topic. It's easy so I thought it would be OK if I squeezed it in.
A clique is a set of nodes in which every pair is linked: the strictest possible definition of a group. A 3-clique is a triangle; a clique has links, and it stays a clique only if every one of them is present, which is why large cliques are rare. That's it... that's all you need to know.
Now for a few more definitions that are less important. A clique is maximal if no node can be added to it; the size of the largest clique in a network is its clique number. Marvel has 1,835 triangles, 477 5-cliques, and a clique number of 8: Betsy Braddock, Cable, Cyclops, Emma Frost, Jean Grey, Northstar, Rachel Summers, and Wolverine, eight X-Men whose pages all link to each other. Finding the largest clique in a big network is NP-hard, but networkx's find_cliques enumerates the maximal ones fast enough for anything in this course.
Cliques are a neat bridge to next week, where we will go deep on the topic of densely interconnected nodes.
Now for the course's most addictive explorable. Clique hunt. Finding 3 cliques is easy. Finding 4 cliques is surprisingly hard (at least for me), and 5 cliques are really, really hard. Once you start playing, you'll begin to find strategies. (Let me know on Teams if I'm the only one who finds this addicting!?)
Each network hides exactly one clique of the size you choose. Click nodes; a violet link means the pair is linked, a dashed line means it is not. Start with the 3-clique, then try the 5-clique, and notice how many promising sets of five fail on one missing link.

Essentials
The Learn core of the week: paths, the machine that computes them, four definitions of importance, the habit of asking "compared to what," and two words for how nodes group: mixing and cliques. All of it is pen-and-paper material; if a line below feels shaky, the section that teaches it is one click up the page.
Walk · path · shortest path
a walk is any sequence of linked nodes; a path never repeats a node; the shortest path between two nodes has the fewest links, and its length is their distance (infinite if no path exists).
Breadth-first search (BFS)
from a start node, visit all nodes at distance 1, then all unvisited nodes at distance 2, and so on. The ring a node lands in is its distance. One run gives the distances from the start to every node.
Eccentricity · diameter · radius · center
a node's eccentricity is its distance to the farthest node; the diameter is the largest eccentricity, the radius the smallest, and the center the node(s) attaining it. Marvel: diameter 6, radius 3, center Spider-Man.
Reachability in directed networks
distances follow the arrows, so and can differ, and one can be infinite. Weakly connected: connected if you ignore direction. Strongly connected: every node reaches every other following the arrows.
Degree centrality
; in directed networks (who is mentioned) and (who mentions) separately. Local and cheap. The question: how many connections?
Closeness centrality
, the inverse mean distance to everyone. High = everything is near. Breaks (goes to 0) if any node is unreachable. The question: how fast can you reach everyone?
Harmonic centrality
with. Closeness that survives disconnected and directed networks.
Betweenness centrality
, the summed fraction of shortest paths between other pairs that pass through; normalized by the number of pairs. High = a broker or bridge; can be high at low degree (the only door to a region) and near zero at high degree (neighbors all linked to each other). The question: who controls the flow?
Eigenvector centrality
: a node is important if its neighbors are important. The leading eigenvector of the adjacency matrix; computed by iterating "replace each score by the sum of the neighbors' scores, rescale."
PageRank
the directed version with a random walker:. Each page splits its vote among its out-links; with probability the walker teleports to a random page, which fixes dangling pages and traps. by convention. The question: who is linked to by the important?
Random walk, stationary distribution
on an undirected network a random walker spends time in proportion to degree,. Random-walk importance is degree unless direction is involved.
Centrality, compared to what
a centrality is only meaningful relative to a null. Against the degree-preserving shuffle, a node whose betweenness matches its shuffled value is "just its degree"; the surprises ( large) are the brokers and the sheltered.
Degree assortativity
the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link, over all links. assortative (hubs link to hubs, typical of social networks), disassortative (hubs link to leaves, typical of technological and biological ones). A star has.
Average neighbor degree
the mean degree of the neighbors of nodes with degree. Rising = assortative, falling = disassortative, flat at = no mixing.
Structural disassortativity
in a heavy-tailed network the hubs must link mostly to small nodes (there are not enough hubs), so appears even under the degree-preserving null. Marvel: real vs shuffled. Compare to the null before calling a network disassortative.
Homophily
assortative mixing by an attribute (team, age, opinion) rather than degree: like links to like. Measured by the attribute assortativity; tested by shuffling the labels across nodes.
Clique · maximal clique · clique number
a set of nodes in which every pair is linked; a clique has links. Maximal = no node can be added. The clique number is the size of the largest clique. A 3-clique is a triangle. Marvel's clique number is 8.

How betweenness is actually computed
The definition sums over all pairs and all shortest paths, which sounds like work or worse. Ulrik Brandes showed in 2001 that one BFS per node is enough. Run BFS from, counting for every node the number of shortest paths from (a node's count is the sum of its predecessors' counts). Then walk back from the farthest ring toward, accumulating each node's share of the paths that pass through it: over the nodes one ring further out that leads to. Add up over all starts and you have the betweenness of every node, in time. This is what networkx runs, and it is why exercise 3.2's "count the shortest paths" is the whole game. For a million-node network even is too slow, so in practice betweenness is estimated from a random sample of start nodes, with an error bar you should report.
Deep dive: eigenvector centrality, PageRank, and how Google got its ranking
Part 5 put eigenvector centrality and PageRank next to each other and asked you to take on faith that they belong together. Here is the long version, which is the best short story in this course: a beautiful idea that does not survive contact with a real directed network, and the two-part repair that turned it into a company.
1 · Eigenvector centrality. The circular definition — you are important if important nodes point at you — becomes an equation the moment you write it down:, or. The scores are an eigenvector of the adjacency matrix and is its eigenvalue. You find it with the power method: start anywhere, multiply by, rescale, repeat. Why that works is one line of algebra. Write the starting vector as a combination of 's eigenvectors; each multiplication scales every component by its own eigenvalue, so the component with the largest eigenvalue outgrows the rest and the others fade. The Perron–Frobenius theorem is what makes the answer trustworthy: for a connected network with non-negative links, the largest eigenvalue is real, positive and unique, and its eigenvector can be chosen with every entry positive — which is the only reason you may read it as a centrality at all. On a disconnected network that guarantee fails, since each component has its own leading eigenvector, and on a bipartite network the iteration oscillates forever instead of settling.
Two facts worth carrying. On the Marvel giant component, and the ranking it produces agrees with plain degree at a rank correlation of: close, but genuinely not the same measure. And on a regular network, where every node has identical degree, eigenvector centrality comes out perfectly uniform — every node scores the same. So the whole difference between eigenvector centrality and degree is a story about degree heterogeneity, and nothing else.
2 · Directed networks, where the adjacency matrix stops working. Now keep the arrows. Importance flows along them: your score is built out of the nodes pointing at you. The trouble is that Perron–Frobenius asks for irreducibility, meaning the network must be strongly connected, and real directed networks are not. Marvel's largest strongly connected component holds 229 of 303 characters. The web is far worse.
What you get instead is a ranking that is mostly zeros, in a very specific pattern: the eigenvector lives on the dominant strongly connected component plus everything downstream of it, and every node upstream scores exactly zero. On the directed Marvel network that prediction is exact. 231 characters score above zero, which is the 229-node component plus the two nodes it feeds, and the remaining 72 are zero. Only 58 characters have no incoming link at all, so the starvation spreads from them.
Worse, the leading eigenvalue of the whole network is just the largest leading eigenvalue among its strongly connected components, so whichever dense cluster wins that contest captures the entire ranking. Page and Brin called these rank sinks, and they are not a theoretical worry. Here is the attack. Build a clique of fake pages, have every one of them also link to a single target, and bolt it onto the real network. Twenty-six fabricated pages make a character with no incoming links whatsoever the single most important node in the network, and the network's leading eigenvalue becomes exactly the farm's own. That is the problem that had to be solved before links could be used to rank anything valuable.
3a · Page and Brin's solution, part one: the walk matrix. Change the matrix. Instead of, use the walk matrix, which is the adjacency matrix with every row divided by that node's out-degree. Each row now sums to one, and is the probability that a walker standing on steps to next. The walker's distribution is a row vector multiplied on the right, so the steady state satisfies: a left eigenvector of with eigenvalue exactly. Contrast eigenvector centrality, a right eigenvector of with eigenvalue. Same word, other side, different matrix. (The walk matrix shares its spectrum with the symmetric, never with itself; the two agree only on regular networks.)
Why is splitting the right move? Three reasons, and only the first is about elegance.
The vote is conserved. Summing lets one page endorse a thousand others at full strength, which is precisely the link farm above. Splitting makes endorsement zero-sum: a page with a thousand out-links gives each of them a thousandth. Look at the table again. The attack that reaches rank 1 against eigenvector centrality only reaches 99th against PageRank.
The scores mean something. They are a probability distribution, summing to one, so they read as where attention actually ends up rather than as an arbitrary positive vector.
It runs at web scale. Every iteration is one sparse matrix–vector product, and you never need to know the eigenvalue, because for a row-normalized matrix it is always.
There is a price, and it is why Part 5 steers you away from PageRank on undirected data. On an undirected network the splitting collapses, and you can see it in two lines. Guess that the walker sits on node with probability, then push the guess through one step: every neighbor hands over its own probability divided by its own degree, the degrees cancel, and what arrives at is simply a count of the links into. The guess reproduces itself, so it is the answer, exactly. Undirected PageRank is degree wearing a hat.
3b · Part two: teleportation. The walk matrix alone does not fix the existence problem. A group of pages linking only to each other still absorbs everything, and a page with no out-links has a row of zeros, which is not a probability distribution at all. So Page and Brin mixed in a constant: with probability the walker ignores the links entirely and jumps to a uniformly random page. Every entry of the resulting Google matrix is strictly positive, which makes it irreducible and aperiodic, which means Perron–Frobenius applies once more and a unique positive stationary distribution exists on any directed network, however ugly.
The teleport buys a second thing for free. The power method's convergence rate is set by the second eigenvalue, and for the Google matrix that is bounded by itself. So is a dial between a sharper ranking and a faster computation, and in 1998, when this had to run across the entire web on cheap hardware, that mattered enormously.
4 · And then Google got to rule the world for a while. The core idea was in the air. Ranking by citation eigenvectors goes back to Katz in 1953 and Bonacich in 1972 in sociometry, and to Pinski and Narin in 1976 for journals, and Kleinberg built HITS at IBM from the same insight in the same year (the Katz and HITS box has the family tree). What Page and Brin had was the pair of repairs above, plus one product decision: PageRank is query-independent. It is computed once, offline, for the whole web, and only then combined with text matching when somebody types a query. HITS ranks a freshly built subgraph per query, which was hopeless at serve time in 1998. Two papers came out of Stanford, The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine and the PageRank Citation Ranking tech report, and search stopped being about the words on the page.
The "for a while" is the honest part. Ranking by links is an adversarial game and the adversaries were good: link farms, paid links, comment spam, an entire industry. Google answered with algorithm after algorithm, stopped publishing per-page PageRank scores, and folded the eigenvector into one signal among hundreds of mostly learned ones. Nobody ranks the web today with a single stationary distribution. The algorithm itself, though, is everywhere else: personalized PageRank drives recommendations, Infomap describes communities as a compressed random walk (week 4), and node2vec turns biased random walks into node embeddings (week 7). You have not seen the last of the walker.
Katz, HITS, and the family tree
Eigenvector centrality and PageRank have relatives. Katz centrality (1953) scores a node by all walks arriving at it, with a walk of length weighted by for some smaller than the inverse leading eigenvalue:, so every node gets a free unit of importance and short walks count more than long ones. It behaves on directed networks where plain eigenvector centrality gives zeros to everything upstream of a dangling node (the deep dive above). HITS (Kleinberg, 1999), built for the web in the same year as PageRank, gives each node two scores: a good hub links to good authorities, and a good authority is linked to by good hubs, iterated together. On the Marvel network, hubs would be the pages that link out to the important characters, authorities the characters they link to; look at the out-degree top ten from Section 3 and guess who the hubs are. Atlas 14.5 has the details.
Hitting times, and what a random walk knows
The random walker is a richer object than PageRank alone. The hitting time is the expected number of steps a walker starting at needs to first reach; it is asymmetric (the walk from a leaf to a hub is short, the reverse is long), which makes it a natural distance for directed or heterogeneous networks. The commute time is symmetric and, remarkably, equals times the effective resistance between and if you treat the network as an electrical circuit with each link a one-ohm resistor. Random-walk quantities like these underlie half of modern network machine learning (the node embeddings you will meet in week 7 are, at heart, statistics of short random walks). Atlas 11.3 and 11.4.
Shuffling the labels instead of the links
Section 7 promised a second kind of null model, and exercise 3.9 asks you to build it. Here is why it is the right one.
Suppose you label every Marvel character X-Men, Avenger or neither, compute the attribute assortativity, and get a healthy positive number. X-Men link to X-Men. Is that a finding? Not yet — you have not said compared to what. Any network in which some group is large, or in which group members happen to be hubs, will produce a positive number without any preference for one's own kind being involved.
The degree-preserving shuffle from week 2 is the wrong tool here. It rewires the links, which destroys exactly the structure you are asking about, and it leaves the labels untouched. So turn it around: freeze the network and permute the labels. Keep every link exactly where it is, take the vector of team labels, shuffle it across the nodes, and recompute. Do that a few hundred times and you have the distribution of attribute assortativity you would see if team membership had nothing to do with who links to whom. Your score against that distribution is the finding.
This null is careful about the right things. The topology is untouched, so degrees, clustering and components cannot explain the result. The number of X-Men is untouched too, because a permutation moves labels around without inventing or destroying any, which matters because a rare label and a common one produce very different baselines. What the permutation destroys is precisely the one thing under test: the association between a node's label and its position.
One trap, and it is the same lesson twice. If the attribute correlates with degree — and in Marvel it will, since the famous X-Men are also the hubs — then a plain permutation breaks that correlation too, and part of your score is just "X-Men are hubs and hubs link to everything." The stricter null is a degree-stratified permutation: sort nodes into degree bins and shuffle labels only within each bin. The labels keep their degree profile, and what survives is homophily beyond popularity. If the two nulls disagree, the gap between them is the part of your effect that degree already explained.
Everything here is a permutation test in the sense of the week 2 Goodies: no distributional assumption, no formula, just the same statistic recomputed on a world where the thing you care about has been switched off. The recipe generalizes past assortativity — any statistic that couples a node attribute to network position, including "do infected nodes cluster" and "do my labels predict community membership," gets tested exactly this way.

One node is responsible. Spider-Man has 106 links in a network of 277 characters, so he touches 38% of the network and owns 7.5% of all its links. Assortative mixing wants his neighbors to look like him, and they cannot: only 37 characters have degree 20 or more (counting him), and 99 have degree 10 or more. Give him the 106 highest-degree partners that exist and their mean degree is, against his own. Those 106 links are pairs no matter how the rest of the network is arranged, and they hold the correlation down by themselves.

So the lopsidedness of the dial is not an artifact of how it rewires. A hub can always find leaves to attach to, which makes disassortative easy; there are never enough other hubs to go around, which makes assortative impossible. The assortative ceiling is not: is where six independent methods stop, but the best bound we can write down by hand is a much weaker, and it is weak because it lets every node claim the top-degree nodes as neighbors at the same time, which no actual graph can arrange. The evidence says the bound is slack rather than the search being stuck, but the claim that is actually supported is "nothing we tried beats," not " is the maximum." Rich clubs, the narrower question
The box above measures how hard the degree sequence caps. The cure is to ask a narrower question. The rich-club coefficient is the density of links among the nodes with degree above, and the interesting quantity is its ratio to the same density in the degree-preserving shuffle: a ratio above one says the top nodes are more tightly wired to each other than their degrees alone require. Scientific collaboration and air-transport networks have strong rich clubs; the Internet, famously, does not. networkx.rich_club_coefficient computes it, and Atlas Ch. 32 Core-Periphery puts it in context. On Marvel, try it: the eight-node X-Men clique is a rich club in miniature.
