Week 2 · Models & null models 303 characters · 1,762 links · 24 years

Growth, and the models that miss it.

Last week: one photograph of the Marvel network. This week: 25 of them, one per year, pulled from Wikipedia's edit history. A picture becomes a process — and a process you can race against a model.

The dataset

Growth models assume degree only ever goes up; Wikipedia deletes links. So: keep only the links that are still there today, date each by the year it first appeared, treat it as permanent. Two rules build every frame.

  • birth_year = max(first_seen, born[source], born[target]) — the year a link first appears, clamped so it can never predate either article. Without the clamp a frame contains links to characters who are not in it. Moved 50 links.
  • A link is in frame y if its birth year is y or earlier. Nothing ever leaves; we assert it frame by frame.

One wrinkle. Frames sample 1 January, but the snapshot was harvested in September 2026 — so 2026 is the network today, not one more equal time step.

Arrival order predicts the hubs

C = 0.713: pick two characters, and the one whose article came first has more links about seven times in ten (p < 0.0002 against 10,000 reshuffles). Hold on to that number — we take it apart below.

How fast does a character grow?

BA predicts trajectories, not final degree: k(t) ∝ (t − ti)β with β = ½. Every trajectory here is monotone, so it is fittable.

0.46median β over 105 characters. BA predicts 0.5
0.57β, first 50 arrivals
0.33β, arrivals 51–150
0.06β after rank 150 — flat

The median is a hit for BA; the rest takes it back. β belongs to the cohort, not the network. Late arrivals do not grow slowly — they do not grow. BA predicts one β; this network has three.

Rich-get-richer is fading

Π(k) is the chance a character with k links gets the next one: for every new link, the target's degree the year before, over how many characters sat at that degree. BA assumes Π(k) ∝ k. We get k0.74 — hubs win, less decisively than BA assumes. Split the record in half and it moves.

The mechanism that built the hubs is measurably weaker than it was. Second-decade links spread over low-degree targets instead of funnelling to the giants — the same era in which the late cohorts stop growing.

The part that surprised us

The player disagrees. Read the C under each panel at 2026. Wikipedia is 0.713 — and random attachment is about 0.70. Not 0.50.

Those references throw the real schedule away: G(n,m) has no growth, textbook BA hands every node six links at once. The player keeps the real arrival order and yearly link budget and changes only the target. Run that way, 60 times each:

Nearly all of the age effect is first-mover advantage. A 2005 article has been collecting links for twenty-one years, a 2020 one for six. That alone gives C ≈ 0.70; preferential attachment adds 0.014. Wikipedia sits inside both intervals. C mostly measures who has had more time — what actually separates this network from a coin flip is the kernel.

"Old articles are just longer"

True, and longer articles hold more links. We wrote this control before looking, and predicted half of it would fail.

It held. Links pointing out are pure prose volume — they vanish under a size control, as they should. Links pointing in do not: two thirds is notability and size, and the rest survives four controls.

So what

  1. The age effect is real and mostly trivial — random attachment on the same schedule reaches 0.701 against the real 0.713.
  2. BA's trajectory prediction holds for the founding cohort only. β falls 0.57 → 0.33 → 0.06: a fast-growing core, a static periphery.
  3. Preferential attachment is real, sublinear and fading — α ≈ 0.88 then ≈ 0.65. This is the part a coin flip cannot produce.
  4. A null model has to match what it is a null for. Same data, same statistic, opposite conclusions.
