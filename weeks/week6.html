Week 6 · From counts to meaning Corpus: the six course week pages

Rare beats frequent.

Six documents, one per week: the course's own pages, turned into plain text by us. We count the words, then ask which of them actually say something about a week: first with TF-IDF, then with a topic model. Both answers depend on choices we made before any formula ran.

Where the text comes from

Everything on this page is built from the course's own six week pages. We did not get a clean text file handed to us. We had to make one, and the choices we made along the way change every number below.

Our data collection pipeline nine steps, and what each one cost

  1. Take only the main text

    Each page has a menu, a table of contents and a footer. We keep only what is inside the page's <main> block, so all of that disappears in one move instead of needing a rule per item.

  2. Pick the parts we want, not the parts we don't

    We ask for paragraphs, list items, headings, glossary entries, quotes and figure captions. Anything else is skipped. Naming what we want is safer than naming what we reject, because a page can always add something new we did not think to exclude.

  3. Make the letters plain

    Curly quotes become straight ones, and accented letters become their closest plain letter, so Barabási becomes Barabasi. This has to happen before the next filters, or don't would end up as don t and 72 author names would be damaged.

  4. Split into sentences, carefully

    We cut at a full stop followed by a space, except after short forms like Fig., Ch., et al. and e.g., which would otherwise be cut in the middle. A plain splitter gets this wrong 92 times.

  5. Throw away every sentence with maths in it

    The pages write maths between dollar signs. We tried removing just the maths and keeping the sentence, and it left wreckage: "Zachary's real split scores." So we drop the whole sentence instead. This costs us 582 sentences, 13.5% of the corpus. That is the most expensive choice on this list.

  6. Keep a small alphabet

    Letters, digits, spaces, and . , ! ? ' - Everything else goes. That removes the leftover brackets, arrows and symbols that maths and code leave behind.

  7. Tidy up, in a fixed order

    Removing text leaves gaps like degree , and doubles like ,,. We close the gaps first, then collapse the doubles. Doing it the other way round leaves new doubles behind. We got this wrong twice before checking properly.

  8. Drop anything left hollow

    If a line is less than half letters after all that, it was never really a sentence. It goes.

  9. Write one piece of text per line

    1,588 lines, each a paragraph, list item or heading that stands on its own.

What this means for the numbers on this page. The 13.5% we dropped is not spread evenly. Week 2 lost 22% of its sentences and week 4 lost 20%, but week 5 lost only 2%. The weeks that used the most maths lost the most text. So when you compare weeks below, remember you are comparing what survived, and the most mathematical weeks survived least. These files hold the pages' language, not their numbers.

Look at the raw counts first

Before any formula, look at what is actually in the text. Here are the ten most common words in each week, just counted, nothing weighted.

Click through the weeks. The top of every list is nearly the same, and nearly useless. Eight of the ten (the, a, and, of, is, to, in, that) are in all six weeks' top ten. Only two content words make any top ten at all: network in weeks 2, 3 and 4, and word in week 6. Counting alone almost cannot tell the network half of the course from the language half.

And it gets worse further down. – words appear in all six weeks. Plain counting puts exactly those words on top, and they cannot separate anything.

Now press Without filler words. The lists come apart at once: edges and nodes for week 1, random, null and distribution for week 2, centrality and betweenness for week 3, modularity and communities for week 4, corpus and token for week 5, topic and tf-idf for week 6. Now none of the ten is shared by all six weeks, where eight were before.

That is tempting, but notice what it took: a list of words we decided in advance did not matter. Those words are 51% of the corpus, and the list is a choice, not a measurement. TF-IDF gets to a similar place without one. A word is only interesting here if it is common in one week and rare in the others, and that can be worked out from the counts themselves. Counting finds common. It does not find rare. The rest of this page is about adding the second half.

Under the book's formula a shared word has almost no chance. It takes the log of the count, so piling up occurrences barely helps, while every extra week that uses the word cuts its idf hard. Take a green word seen three times, and a word that appears in three weeks trying to tie it. Under the book's formula the shared word needs 659 occurrences. Under the course formula it needs 8, about 2.6 times the green word's three. That is why the book's list is green from top to bottom in every week.

This is probably mostly down to our small corpus. With only six documents, idf can take just six values, and log10(6/df) drops to almost nothing after a few weeks. With hundreds of documents, sharing a word with a handful of others would hardly matter. Even so, both lists do a good job of pulling out what each week is about: pagerank, closeness and eigenvector for week 3, philosophers, partition and dendrogram for week 4, tokenization and subword for week 5, topics, embedding and idf for week 6.

How alike are the weeks?

The matrix splits the course in two. The first four weeks, all about networks, are more alike among themselves (0.07 to 0.16) than with weeks 5 and 6 (0.02 to 0.05). Weeks 5 and 6 are by far the most alike pair at 0.33, which is what we would expect, since week 6 builds further on the NLP from week 5 and keeps using its words: corpus, word, language. The book shows the same pattern, only weaker, because it gives less TF-IDF weight to words that occur in several weeks. Those shared words are exactly what pushes the similarities higher under the course formula.

What each week is about

TF-IDF scores one word at a time. It never says a week has a theme. For that we use a topic model, LDA, which looks for groups of words that keep turning up together.

One change first. For TF-IDF we treated each week as a single document, so N = 6. Six documents is far too few to find topics in. The model needs to see words appear together in some documents and not in others, and with six there is almost nothing to compare. So we cut the weeks into pieces of about 200 words each, which gives 238 documents. Each piece remembers which week it came from, but the model is never told.

Then we ask for 6, 7 and 8 topics and see where each week's text lands.

Six topics does not give you the six weeks. At K = 6, weeks 5 and 6 both land on the same topic, word words context corpus text vectors, taking 63% and 68% of their text. Five topics end up covering six weeks. The two language weeks share so much vocabulary that telling them apart is what costs an extra topic.

At K = 7 all six weeks finally get their own topic, and week 6's becomes document word words matrix context idf at 76%. At K = 8 the weeks still separate. Weeks 1 to 4 each hold one topic the whole way through: power laws, null models, centrality, communities. But only week 4 holds it firmly, at 61 to 67% of its text with nothing else close. Week 3 is the shakiest: at six topics its own topic takes 42% and the runner-up takes 40%, which is close to a coin toss.

The second column needs a word of explanation. NMI compares two ways of grouping the same 238 pieces of text: the week a piece actually came from, and the topic it leans on most. A score of 1 would mean the two groupings are identical. A score of 0 would mean knowing one tells you nothing at all about the other. We land near the middle.

A topic is not a subject

It is tempting to read the grid above as "week 4 is about communities". That is not what a topic model says, and the gap matters.

What LDA actually assumes. A topic is a probability distribution over words, nothing more. It is a long list of every word in the vocabulary with a number saying how likely that word is under this topic. And a document is not in a topic; it is a mixture of all of them, a set of fractions that add up to 1. When we say week 4 "is" the communities topic, we mean 61% of its text leans that way. The other 39% is spread across the rest.

So the labels we wrote are ours, not the model's. LDA gave us communities community links link modularity groups. Calling that "the communities topic" is a summary we chose, and summarising a distribution over a thousand words with six of them throws nearly all of it away.

Three reasons to stay careful

  • The number of topics is something we chose. Six, seven and eight all gave defensible answers, and they disagree about whether weeks 5 and 6 are one subject or two. Nothing in the data decides it.
  • The usual quality score points the wrong way. Perplexity, the standard measure, keeps improving as we ask for fewer topics, and is best at four, which merges whole pairs of weeks. We would have picked a worse model by trusting it.
  • Agreement with the real weeks never gets close. Across every setting from 4 to 10 topics, NMI stays between 0.46 and 0.54, and purity between 0.50 and 0.63. The topics clearly track the course, and they clearly are not the course.

And the text itself is a choice. We dropped every sentence with maths in it, which cost week 2 and week 4 a fifth of their sentences while week 5 lost almost nothing. The weeks that look thinnest here are partly the weeks our own pipeline cut the most. A topic model cannot tell the difference between a subject that was not discussed and one we deleted.

Make the scale balance

Both words sit in the same week, so they share the same |d|. The only things that differ are the count and the idf.

  • The green word appears in this week only, so df = 1 and its idf is ln(6/1) ≈ 1.79, the highest any word can get. You see its full TF-IDF.
  • The orange word also appears in other weeks (the filled dots). The more weeks share it, the smaller its idf gets. A word in all six weeks gets ln(6/6) = 0, and no count can save it.
  • Pick the count that would give the orange word the same TF-IDF as the green one. One of the four options is right; the others are close enough to be worth thinking about.
  • Since |d| cancels out, the exact answer is
    countorange = countgreen × ln(6/1) ÷ ln(6/df)
    The right option scores 100. A wrong one still scores something, based on how close it was.
  • With Course + book you pick once per formula. The book takes the log of the count, so its answers run into powers of ten. A normal option there is 6.9 × 10⁷.

After each round you also see how often the orange word really appears in that week, the TF-IDF that real count gives it in that week's text, and whether that is enough to beat the green one.
